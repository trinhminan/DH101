# Week 1 – Reverse Engineering

## The Artifact
This week's artifact explores machine-generated identity through [This Person Does Not Exist](https://thispersondoesnotexist.com/), a generative adversarial network (GAN) that creates photorealistic images of people who have never lived. Each refresh produces a new face—complete with subtle imperfections that make it feel almost real.

The tool reveals how AI learns patterns of human appearance by reverse-engineering the visual grammar of faces: the distribution of features, lighting, skin tone, and expression. By engaging with the site repeatedly, I noticed how the "uncanny valley" effect weakens—the more I view generated faces, the less strange they feel, and the more I question what authenticity means when perfection is artificially constructed.

**Explore it yourself:** [https://thispersondoesnotexist.com/](https://thispersondoesnotexist.com/)

## Process Notes
I chose This Person Does Not Exist because it provokes immediate questions about making, authenticity, and labor. Rather than creating an artifact myself, I'm using an existing tool as a critical instrument—asking what it means to "make" with AI-generated media.

The decision to reverse-engineer its process involved researching GANs, discriminator/generator networks, and training datasets. I spent time interacting with the tool, documenting patterns (age ranges, diversity, recurring features), and considering the ethics of synthetic faces.

Key decisions:
- Use an existing AI tool as the artifact rather than something I "made by hand"
- Focus on the philosophical question: is using an AI tool making, or curating?
- Document the uncanny experience of consuming infinite synthetic identities

## Reflection
Week 1 asks: What counts as "making" in the age of AI? What might be gained/lost when machines participate in making?

Using This Person Does Not Exist challenges traditional definitions of making. I didn't paint, code, or construct these faces—the algorithm did. Yet my act of engaging with the tool, selecting which faces to revisit, and writing about the experience creates meaning. In that sense, making becomes curation and interpretation rather than craftsmanship.

What I gain: access to infinite visual possibility, instant exploration of pattern and variation, a tool for thought. What I lose: the material friction of handmaking, the embodied knowledge that comes from struggling with resistance, the accountability that comes with direct authorship.

The deeper issue is labor. Every face on this site was learned from millions of real faces scraped without consent. The "making" is distributed across annotators who labeled training data, engineers who built the model, and the artists whose work trained the system. So making with AI is not neutral—it abstracts and obscures the human work beneath the surface.
