## Process Notes

### Research Documentation

This digital mapping project investigates the physical geography of artificial intelligence by mapping the locations of data centers and infrastructure used by major technology companies such as Google and Meta across North America, Europe, and Asia. The primary conceptual source for this project is Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI*, which argues that artificial intelligence should be understood as a material system rather than a purely virtual technology. Crawford explains that AI depends on natural resources, human labor, energy systems, and large-scale infrastructures. This framework helped shape the central idea of this map: instead of focusing on algorithms or software, the project highlights the physical locations that make AI systems possible.

In addition to the *Atlas of AI*, the research also cited environmental reports, such as Greenpeace's "Click Clean Virginia," which examines the energy consumption and environmental impact of large data centers. These sources helped provide context for why technology companies build data centers in particular regions, often choosing locations with access to affordable electricity, available land, and strong network infrastructure.

The most difficult part of this research was identifying the exact locations and details of many data centers. Technology companies rarely provide complete public information about their infrastructure. While they often promote sustainability initiatives, specific details about energy use, water consumption, and computing capacity are much harder to locate. Information about data centers is often scattered across corporate announcements, environmental reports, and investigative journalism.

This lack of transparency reflects an important idea discussed in *Atlas of AI*: the infrastructure behind AI is often hidden from public view (Crawford, 2021, p. 42). The metaphor of "the cloud" suggests something abstract or immaterial, but in reality, these systems depend on large industrial facilities that consume significant energy and resources.

One of the most surprising findings from the mapping process was the geographic concentration of AI infrastructure. Even though AI technologies are used globally, many of the largest data centers are located in the United States and parts of Europe. This reveals how technological power and infrastructure are concentrated in a relatively small number of regions.

Another surprising aspect was the scale of the physical infrastructure required to support AI systems. Data centers require massive amounts of electricity, cooling systems, and land in order to operate continuously (Crawford, 2021, p. 48). Mapping these locations made it clear that AI is not simply a digital technology but part of a much larger physical and environmental system.

## Reflection

### Artist Statement

This map visualizes the hidden geography of artificial intelligence infrastructure. AI is often described as something abstract or existing "in the cloud," but in reality it depends on large physical systems located around the world. By mapping the locations of data centers operated by companies such as Google and Meta, this project highlights the material infrastructure that supports AI technologies.

When these locations are viewed together globally, a clear pattern emerges. Many major data centers are concentrated in the United States and parts of Europe, where access to land, electricity, and digital infrastructure makes large-scale computing possible. This geographic concentration reflects existing centers of technological and economic power, showing that the development and control of AI are not evenly distributed.

The map argues that AI is not only a technological system but also a geographic and political one. While the benefits of AI technologies are experienced globally through digital platforms and online services, the infrastructure that powers these systems requires large amounts of energy, land, and resources. These physical costs are often invisible to the everyday users of AI.

This project connects to the ideas of *Data Feminism* by focusing on questions of power and visibility. Mapping AI infrastructure makes visible the systems that support digital technologies and challenges the idea that data systems are neutral or immaterial. Instead, it reveals how technological systems reflect broader global inequalities.

If this map were created from another perspective—such as that of local communities or workers maintaining these facilities—it might emphasize environmental impacts, labor conditions, and other social consequences that remain largely hidden in mainstream narratives about AI.
