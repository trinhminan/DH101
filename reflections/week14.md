# Week 14 Reflection: What does it mean to be human in the age of AI?

In the age of generative AI, the traditional markers of humanity—the ability to write an essay, draft a memo, or illustrate a concept—have been decoupled from the human mind. When machine learning models can produce a passing bar exam score or a "passable, if dull" undergraduate essay in seconds, we are forced to ask what remains of our unique position as creators. My reflection over this semester has led to a specific conclusion: being human in the age of AI means moving beyond being a mere "producer of content" to becoming a "critical maker" who embraces the moral accountability of intent and the friction of materiality. Humanity is not found in the efficiency of the output, but in the "care" invested in the process.

Earlier this semester, when we explored the risks of "stochastic parrots," I initially felt a sense of existential dread. Bender et al. (2021) describe large language models (LLMs) as systems for "haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms" without any access to meaning or "communicative intent" (Bender et al., 2021). This resonated with my experience in the "Generation" make, where I prompted ChatGPT to analyze a manual that did not exist. As Myra Hoover noted in her reflection, the AI dutifully produced professional-sounding nonsense despite having no manual to analyze. It became clear that to be human is to possess the accountability that a machine lacks; as Maggie Nelson noted, "Words change depending on who speaks them; there is no cure" (Bender et al., 2021). The machine can simulate the form of human thought, but it cannot stand behind the truth of its utterances.

This realization shifted my perspective during our "Selfie" and "Comic" makes. In the age of AI, where image generators can create 15 billion images in a single year—more than Shutterstock's entire library—the act of drawing by hand can feel "amateur" or even obsolete. However, following Matt Ratto's (2011) framework, I realized that "critical making" is not about the aesthetic quality of the object, but the "creation of novel understandings by the makers themselves" (Ratto, 2011). When I hand-crafted my material "selfie" rather than using an AI filter, I experienced what Ratto calls the "push-back" of the material—the "resistance of reality to our attempts to contain it" (Ratto, 2011). This friction is essentially human. While an AI system can produce an image with "310 and 2900 times less CO2e" than a human illustrator (Tomlinson et al., 2024), it cannot "unlearn" the creative anxiety of a blank page or "care for" the socio-technical implications of its own existence.

Furthermore, being human in this era requires a commitment to "procedural literacy" as a form of resistance. Johnson and Salter (2025) argue that we must understand the "digital language" that underlies the interface, which is often designed to "evade the user's gaze" (Johnson and Salter, 2025). My work with the Tracery bot assignment forced me to stop "caring about" AI as a vague threat and start "caring for" the specific biases encoded in its grammars. I saw firsthand how AI authorship is often an "uncredited remix" of human labor. By documenting the "documentation debt" that larger models incur—the vast, uncurated datasets that overrepresent hegemonic viewpoints—I began to see that being human involves the ethical choice to work at a scale where we can remain accountable for our data (Bender et al., 2021).

Ultimately, the argument that AI is more "efficient" or "environmentally sound" ignores the fundamental truth that humans continue to exist and "emit carbon whether or not they are engaged in writing/drawing" (Tomlinson et al., 2024). Our humanity is not a cost to be optimized. To be human in the age of AI is to be a "cautious Prometheus," intentionally slowing down to reconnect materiality and morality (Ratto, 2011). We are the ones who must bridge the gap between "thinking" and "making," ensuring that our technologies remain "intrinsically political, contentious and open to discussion" (Ratto, 2011). In the end, the machine may provide the "stochastic" output, but only the human provides the "care".
