# Week 5 Reflection

Reflect 5: How does AI alter authorship and remix culture? Who owns AI-made art?

## Key Insight
AI doesn't destroy authorship—it scrambles it. Remix culture thrived on the assumption that recontextualization transforms meaning. Sampling a drum break into a new song? You own the *creative choice*, even though you didn't create the raw material. AI changes the economics of this. When an image generator produces 10,000 variations based on a text prompt, who's the author? The person who wrote the prompt. The engineer who built the model. The artists whose work trained it. The corporations that collected the data. Ownership becomes fractional, muddied, impossible to attribute cleanly. Legal systems designed around individual authorship collapse. The honest answer: everyone and no one. The remix tradition assumed transparency—you could see the source material, credit it, transform it visibly. AI breaks that transparency. The "author" of an AI-generated image can't credit the training data; it's too distributed. This isn't remix anymore; it's more like *statistical recombination*. For remix culture to survive AI, we need new attribution models: metadata that traces data sources, legal frameworks that acknowledge distributed creation, and norms that treat AI art-making as collaborative by default rather than individual by default.

## Example
A GIF remix of a movie scene? I can see the original, understand the creative choice. An AI-generated face? I can't trace which training faces contributed to it, so remix credit becomes meaningless.

## What I'm Learning
- Ownership is increasingly a legal fiction rather than a lived reality
- Remix culture needs new norms for the AI era
- Transparency about training data is the key to ethical AI art
