# Week 11 Reflection

Reflect 11: Who does the invisible work of AI, and who profits from it?

## Key Insight
AI's invisibility is built on layers of *visible* human labor that's been rendered invisible. Somewhere, people are labeling images for machine learning, verifying content, moderating toxicity, designing datasets. This work is often outsourced to countries with lower wages and weaker labor protections. The person in the Global South labeling 10,000 faces earns pennies; the company licenses that labeled data to build a billion-dollar model; shareholders profit. The labor is visible in its *absence*—in the seamless functioning of a system that required 500 human decisions per model. Who profits? Shareholders, corporations, governments with surveillance capabilities, wealthy users who can afford the premium version. Who does the work? People with fewer options, often migrant workers, in precarious positions. The invisibility is intentional. It's easier to market AI as "intelligent automation" than to show workers hunched over screens, making micro-decisions for microscopic pay. The markup from $0.02/label to a $10B valuation is spectacular, and that gap is where injustice lives. Confronting this means making visible: tracking where your data was labeled, researching which companies employ ethical practices, demanding transparency in model pipelines. It means recognizing that *every* intelligent system sits atop a pyramid of invisible labor, and asking whether that pyramid is sustainable or just.

## Example
An image recognition model that seems magical? Behind it: 50,000 images labeled by contract workers earning below minimum wage, working in offices with poor conditions, with no job security or benefits.

## What I'm Learning
- Intelligence is built on invisible labor
- Economic extraction happens through algorithmic obscurity
- Ethical AI means making labor visible and compensating it fairly
