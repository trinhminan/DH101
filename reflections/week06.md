# Week 6 Reflection

Reflect 6: What do we gain/lose when machines “read” literature for us?
## Key Insight
Machine reading trades depth for scale. When humans read, we *feel* — we notice the rasp in a character's voice, anticipate plot turns through emotional intuition, miss things because we're tired or preoccupied. Machines notice statistical patterns: word frequency, semantic clusters, linguistic structures invisible to human attention. This is gain and loss simultaneously. Gain: we can now analyze patterns across 10,000 novels in seconds; we spot historical trends in language and representation. Loss: we outsource the intimate act of *interpretation*. Reading isn't just information retrieval; it's a practice of attention and meaning-making. When a machine summarizes *Anna Karenina*, it might extract plot points perfectly but miss the *feel* of Russian alienation, the way Tolstoy's digressions create meaning. The real danger isn't that machines read *instead* of humans, but that we might *trust* machine readings as complete. They're not. They're *augmentations*—useful for asking new questions but dangerous as replacements for human engagement. We need both: the machine's pattern recognition and the human's interpretive depth. The question isn't whether to use AI analysis, but whether we remain conscious that we're using a *particular kind* of reading—useful for certain questions, blind to others.

## Example
A machine can tell you that word "light" appears 247 times in a novel, tracking its semantic drift. But it won't understand the *hope* embedded in that word's recurrence or how it feels to *live* in that novel's darkness.

## What I'm Learning
- Different reading methods reveal different things
- Machine reading is a tool, not a replacement for human interpretation
- The real risk is forgetting what we lose when we outsource reading
